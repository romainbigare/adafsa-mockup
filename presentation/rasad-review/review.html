Wafra Review
Reviewed 30 September 2026 by Romain Bigare, Director of Digital Systems
One

The menu does not show what the client is buying

What we found

The side menu has 8 items. 4 of them are the work: Executive View, Farm, Farm Monitoring and Reports. The other 4 are support: Raise Ticket, Contact Us, Feedback, and Account and Settings.

The 6 modules in the contract do not appear in the menu. They sit as 6 small cards in the middle of the Farm page, with one number each. Nobody can go straight to Irrigation Efficiency, and a new user cannot tell from the menu what the platform measures.

What we suggest

We would make the menu a list of what the client pays for. Overview sits at the top. The 6 modules follow, by name, and each one opens into its own pages: one for what exists now, one for what has changed. Individual Farms and Reports come next. The 4 support items become one Support entry at the foot of the menu. Every page has its own link, so a view can be sent to a colleague.

Two

One page carries every level of detail

What we found

The Farm page is about 5,400 pixels tall: six screens of scrolling. It mixes the whole emirate, one farm picked from a menu, and 15 farms ranked as large cards. The Executive View and Farm Monitoring each bring a layout of their own, so a user does not know where to look first.

What we suggest

We would split the product into 3 levels. The Overview shows the emirate, or one province. A module page shows one topic across all farms, then the farms behind it. A farm page shows one farm across all topics. Every page is built from the same 3 blocks: a strip of figures, one chart or map, and one table. Lists of farms become table rows that can be sorted, searched and exported, since 15 cards already fill two screens and the contract covers more than 25,000 farms.

Three

The first page mixes what we have with how it is doing

What we found

The Executive View opens with the right kind of numbers: farms, fields, trees, cultivated, fallow and barren land, structures. It then adds a canopy health bar for the whole emirate, and draws farm counts as red bubbles, which read as an alarm.

At the level of the emirate, the question is production capacity: what is grown, where, and on how much land. Health belongs to a farm and its inspector. There is also no crop table, so "how many farms grow tomatoes this year, on how many dunums?" has no answer on the first page.

Left: farm counts drawn in red. Right: a health bar for the whole emirate, on the first page.
What we suggest

We would keep the first page to an inventory: 4 figures (farms, farm area, land in production, crops growing this month), a map of plain grey bubbles that count farms and split as you zoom, and 2 tables: crops by area, and crops by number of farms. Each row opens from a group to its crops. Share bars run against 100%, so the grey part is the rest of the answer. Health moves to the module pages and the farm page.

Four

The filters do not reach the data

What we found

The header holds a region menu, and some lists carry region chips of their own. Other screens have no filter at all. There is no way to filter by crop or tree type, so the share bars and tables always show every type at once.

No list opens from a crop group to its crops, or from a crop to the farms that grow it. A simple question — how many farms grow tomatoes in Al Ain, and which ones — has no answer on any screen.

The header: a region menu and coverage labels, with no crop or tree filter.
What we suggest

We would put one filter bar under the title of every page. It holds the region — the emirate, one of the 3 provinces, then the farm centre — and one chip per group, in a fixed order: cereals, fodder, open field, date palm, fruit trees, forest trees. The chip's name turns its group on or off. Its arrow opens the crops or varieties inside.

The bar is live. The figures, the chart, the map and every table on the page change together, with no apply button. The filter is kept in the page link. Each page offers only the chips that change its answer, since a switch that moves nothing is worse than no switch. Every summary row opens into its parts, and every farm name opens the farm.

Five

Everything is shown as "now"

What we found

Every number describes today. No figure says whether it went up or down since last quarter or last year, there is no chart over time, and no list of the farms that changed. For an agency, the direction of travel is often the point: is fodder shrinking, is more land left fallow, are farms irrigating better than a year ago? The contract also names a seasonal change report, annual tree change and a quarterly irrigation trend.

4 figures for a farm, with no sense of direction.
What we suggest

We would make 2 changes. Every headline figure carries its change and a small trend line, against last quarter or last year, chosen with one switch. And every module gets a Change page beside its Now page: a chart over time, the biggest movers, and the farms behind the movement, oldest to newest. Crops, irrigation and yield use 6 quarters, the smallest window with both a last-quarter and a last-year reading. Trees and structures use 3 years, because they move slowly. Change pages carry no map. Until enough history exists, the page says so plainly.

Six

The farm page is written for a scientist

What we found

Farm Monitoring opens on satellite codes (S1, S2, S3), cloud cover to two decimals, pass times to the second, and NDVI, NDWI and SAVI switches. An inspector preparing a visit wants to know what the farm grows, how it is doing, and what to check. None of that is written in plain words.

The farm cards also give farmer instructions, such as "Increase pivot delivery by 12% between 19:00 and 04:00". Watering schedules, soil salinity and weather belong in the farmer's app. The contract sells these modules without prescriptive irrigation advice.

The first thing a user reads about a farm: satellite passes and cloud cover.
What we suggest

We would give each farm 2 pages in plain words, and one closed tab for experts. The profile gives the area, province and farm centre, what the farm grows now, its trees, its irrigation efficiency, its water this month, and what changed since last quarter. The second page lists what to check on a visit, as neutral findings, most important first, with a print button. The satellite detail — passes, indices and the field table — moves into a tab that stays closed until someone opens it. Nothing is lost; it moves one level deeper.

Seven

The scores use their own scales

What we found

Irrigation efficiency is shown in 4 bands: Excellent, Good, Moderate and Low, so a farm at 78 shows as Good. Canopy health is an NDVI percentage. Water is flagged as overdraft as soon as the balance goes below zero. The contract defines all 3 differently, and each drives a real decision: who keeps a subsidy, which trees are stressed, which farm uses too much water. With two scales, the same farm is judged twice.

"Canopy health (NDVI)" and one-word labels on the ranking cards.
What we suggest

We would define each score once, as the contract does, and let every screen read that one definition: the name, the band edges, the words and the colours. Irrigation efficiency gets 5 bands and a line at 65 for subsidy, with a list of the farms on each side. The Canopy Health Index gets one number per farm for palms and one for fruit trees. Over-allocation is flagged each month when use is more than 25% above crop demand, and broken down by crop to show where the water goes.

Eight

The screens are loud

What we found

A single screen uses green, teal, blue, orange, yellow, purple and red, often with no meaning: the small caption under each figure has its own colour. Dark teal headers sit next to white cards. Almost every number is in a coloured pill, every section opens with an icon in a tinted square, and many labels are capitals at 9 or 10 pixels.

Titles are long and abstract. Some lines claim things that nothing on screen supports. Together these make the product look generated rather than designed, and an official will ask what the claims mean.

A section title, and a status line in the menu.
What we suggest

We would adopt a short set of rules, and hold every screen to them.

Bringing it together

Every page in one frame

Each screen appears to have been designed on its own, so each brings its own layout, its own boxes and its own colours. We would place every page in the same frame. That settles most of the 8 points above at once, and it makes each new page cheaper to build. The frame also keeps what already works well: the English and Arabic switch, the search box, the Reports page, and a date on the data.

The frame has 4 parts. The top bar holds what stays true everywhere: the region, one search box for farms, the language switch and the account. Choosing a region once holds as somebody moves between pages.

The rail on the left holds the Overview, the 6 modules, Individual Farms and Reports, with Support at the foot.

A context line sits under the bar on every page. It gives the region, the number of farms, the area and the date of the data, so somebody always knows what they are looking at and how fresh it is.

The page itself starts with its title and the filter bar, then the same 3 blocks every time: a strip of figures, a chart or a map, and a table. Once those exist, a new module is a set of figures, one chart and one table, rather than another page to design. Because the frame is one piece, it also turns right-to-left for Arabic as one piece.
