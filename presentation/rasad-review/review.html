Wafra Review
Reviewed 30 September 2026 by Romain Bigare, Director of Digital Systems
One

Giving the six modules a place in the menu

What we found

The side menu has 8 items. Executive View, Farm, Farm Monitoring and Reports carry the work, and the other 4 are support: Raise Ticket, Contact Us, Feedback, and Account and Settings.

The 6 modules named in the contract are reached through cards on the Farm page, with one number each. A module has no page of its own, so there is no place to show all of its deliverables, and a new user cannot tell from the menu what the platform measures.

What we suggest

We would give each module its own entry in the menu, with a small group of pages under it. The existing screens keep their place: the Executive View stays at the top, Farm Monitoring becomes the page for one farm, and Reports stays the place for exports. The 4 support items can share one Support entry at the foot of the menu.

Two

Separating the levels of detail

What we found

The Farm page gathers a lot of useful content: land use, crop and tree shares, the 6 module cards, and a ranking of farms. It is about 5,400 pixels tall, or six screens of scrolling, and it moves between the whole emirate, one farm picked from a menu, and a list of 15 farms. A reader has to work out, block by block, which level each part is about.

What we suggest

We would keep the existing screens and give each one a single level. The Executive View covers the emirate or one province. The new module pages cover one topic across all farms. Farm Monitoring covers one farm. Almost everything on the Farm page already has a home in that scheme, so the work is mostly moving blocks rather than building them.

The ranking is worth one more change. As rows in a table rather than cards, the same farms can be sorted, searched and exported, and the list still works at 25,000 farms.

Three

Keeping the Executive View about capacity

What we found

The Executive View opens with the right numbers for an agency: farms, fields, trees, and cultivated, fallow and barren land. Its tabs for open agriculture, protected agriculture and structures are a clear way in. Two elements pull it toward health instead: a canopy health bar for the whole emirate, and farm counts drawn as red bubbles, which read as an alarm.

The cards under Open Agriculture also stop at the group. Cultivated Fields names its crops as a line of text, so "how many farms grow tomatoes, on how many dunums?" has no answer on the page.

Left: farm counts drawn in red. Right: a health bar for the whole emirate.
What we suggest

We would keep the page and its tabs, and make 3 changes. The canopy health bar moves to Tree Monitoring, where health is the subject. The farm-count bubbles take a neutral colour, since they count rather than judge. And each card under Open Agriculture opens into its crops, with the area and the number of farms for each.

Four

Letting the filters reach every page

What we found

The header already holds a region menu, which is the right place for it. Some lists add region chips of their own, and there is no filter for crop or tree type, so the share bars and tables always show every type at once.

No list opens from a crop group to its crops, or from a crop to the farms that grow it. A simple question — how many farms grow tomatoes in Al Ain, and which ones — has no answer on any screen.

The header today: a region menu, coverage labels, search and the language switch.
What we suggest

We would build on the header. Next to the region menu, a second menu picks crop and tree types: the groups in a fixed order — cereals, fodder, open field, date palm, fruit trees, forest trees — each opening into its crops or varieties. It can take the place of the coverage labels.

Both menus then apply to every page, and to every figure, chart, map and table on it at once, with no apply button. The choice is kept in the page link and holds as somebody moves between pages, so the chips on single lists are no longer needed. Summary rows open into their parts, and every farm name opens the farm.

Five

Adding the direction of travel

What we found

Every number describes today. No figure says whether it went up or down since last quarter or last year, there is no chart over time, and no list of the farms that changed. For an agency, the direction of travel is often the point: is fodder shrinking, is more land left fallow, are farms irrigating better than a year ago? The contract also names a seasonal change report, annual tree change and a quarterly irrigation trend.

4 figures for a farm, with no sense of direction.
What we suggest

We would make 2 changes. Every existing figure, on the Executive View, the module cards and Farm Monitoring, carries its change and a small trend line, against last quarter or last year, chosen with one switch. And every module gets a Change view, as a page or a tab: a chart over time, the biggest movers, and the farms behind the movement, oldest to newest. Crops, irrigation and yield use 6 quarters, the smallest window with both a last-quarter and a last-year reading. Trees and structures use 3 years, because they move slowly. Change pages carry no map. Until enough history exists, the page says so plainly.

Six

Writing the farm page for an inspector

What we found

Farm Monitoring brings a lot together for one farm: health, yield, irrigation, water and a field table. It opens, though, on satellite codes (S1, S2, S3), cloud cover to two decimals, pass times to the second, and NDVI, NDWI and SAVI switches. An inspector preparing a visit wants to know what the farm grows, how it is doing, and what to check. That is hard to find in plain words.

The farm cards also give farmer instructions, such as "Increase pivot delivery by 12% between 19:00 and 04:00". Watering schedules, soil salinity and weather belong in the farmer's app. The contract sells these modules without prescriptive irrigation advice.

The first thing a user reads about a farm: satellite passes and cloud cover.
What we suggest

We would keep the page and change its order. It opens on a plain profile: the 4 figures already there, what the farm grows, its trees, and what changed since last quarter. A second tab lists what to check on a visit, as neutral findings, most important first, with a print button. The satellite strip, the indices and the field table move into a Satellite detail tab, kept for the specialists who need them. Nothing is lost; it moves one level deeper.

Seven

Aligning the scores with the contract

What we found

Irrigation efficiency is shown in 4 bands: Excellent, Good, Moderate and Low, so a farm at 78 shows as Good. Canopy health is an NDVI percentage. Water is flagged as overdraft as soon as the balance goes below zero. The contract defines all 3 differently, and each drives a real decision: who keeps a subsidy, which trees are stressed, which farm uses too much water. With two scales, the same farm is judged twice.

"Canopy health (NDVI)" and one-word labels on the ranking cards.
What we suggest

We would define each score once, as the contract does, and let every screen read that one definition: the name, the band edges, the words and the colours. Irrigation efficiency gets 5 bands and a line at 65 for subsidy, with a list of the farms on each side. The Canopy Health Index gets one number per farm for palms and one for fruit trees. Over-allocation is flagged each month when use is more than 25% above crop demand, and broken down by crop to show where the water goes.

Eight

A calmer visual language

What we found

The product has a clear identity: the ADAFSA lockup, a bilingual name and a steady green. Around it, a single screen uses green, teal, blue, orange, yellow, purple and red, often with no meaning: the small caption under each figure has its own colour. Dark teal headers sit next to white cards. Almost every number is in a coloured pill, every section opens with an icon in a tinted square, and many labels are capitals at 9 or 10 pixels.

Titles are long and abstract. Some lines claim things that nothing on screen supports. Together they make the product feel busier than it is, and an official will ask what the claims mean.

A section title, and a status line in the menu.

Bringing it together

Working from a common page frame

Each screen has so far been designed on its own, so each brings its own layout, its own boxes and its own colours. We would place every page in one shared frame. That settles most of the 8 points above at once, and it makes each new page cheaper to build. Much of the frame already exists: the header holds the region menu, the search box, the English and Arabic switch and the account, and the Reports page is the natural home for exports.

The frame has 4 parts. The top bar holds what stays true everywhere: the region and the crop menus side by side, one search box for farms, the language switch and the account. A choice made there holds as somebody moves between pages.

The rail on the left holds the Executive View, the 6 modules, Farm Monitoring and Reports, with Support at the foot. Any of the menu orders in point One fits here.

A context line sits under the bar on every page. It repeats the selection and gives the number of farms, the area and the date of the data, so somebody always knows what they are looking at and how fresh it is.

The page itself follows the same 3 blocks every time: a strip of figures, a chart or a map, and a table. Once those exist, a new module is a set of figures, one chart and one table, rather than another page to design. Because the frame is one piece, it also turns right-to-left for Arabic as one piece.
